# Controlled Environment Chamber (CEC)

## Product Overview

The Eden Industrial Systems Controlled Environment Chamber (CEC) is a self-contained, benchtop clean-air enclosure designed to create a localized controlled environment within an existing cleanroom or controlled manufacturing space.

The CEC is intended for applications where sensitive components, assemblies, samples, tools, or work-in-process require a cleaner environment than the surrounding room can provide. For example, a CEC may be installed at a workstation located in an ISO Class 7 or ISO Class 8 cleanroom to provide a localized environment designed for a higher cleanliness classification, potentially including ISO Class 5 when configured, validated, and operated accordingly.

Room air is drawn into the top of the unit, filtered through a HEPA filtration system, and supplied to the chamber. The airflow system is designed to continuously sweep the protected interior with filtered air before exhausting air from the chamber through designated lower-level outlets.

The CEC is the flagship product within the Eden Industrial Systems clean-environment product line.

> **Important:** ISO cleanliness classifications and recovery performance are measurable system-level claims. No ISO Class 5 or other ISO classification should be advertised for a production model until the final design has been tested and validated using the applicable ISO 14644 requirements and defined operating conditions.

---

## Product Concept

The CEC addresses a simple problem:

A facility may not need—or may not economically justify—maintaining an entire room at the cleanliness level required by its most sensitive process or component.

Instead, the CEC creates a smaller protected zone directly at or near the operator's workstation.

A representative use case is:

1. An operator works at a workstation inside an ISO Class 7 or ISO Class 8 cleanroom.
2. A sensitive component requires greater environmental protection during interruptions, staging, inspection, or other portions of the process.
3. The operator places the component inside the CEC.
4. The chamber doors are closed.
5. HEPA-filtered airflow continuously purges the chamber.
6. The component remains within the localized controlled environment until it is needed again.

This allows higher-level environmental control to be applied to a relatively small volume rather than the entire surrounding room.

## Airflow Concept

The airflow system is the primary functional system of the CEC.

The preliminary airflow path is:

```text
Surrounding Room Air
        |
        v
    Air Intake
        |
        v
   Pre-Filter*
        |
        v
   Blower / Fan
        |
        v
    HEPA Filter
        |
        v
Filtered Supply Air
        |
        v
 Controlled Chamber
        |
        v
Lower-Level Exhaust
        |
        v
 Surrounding Room
```

`*` Pre-filter use and location are to be determined during engineering.

The final arrangement of the blower, HEPA filter, pre-filter, plenum, and airflow controls will be determined through engineering analysis and prototype testing.

---

## Airflow Design Objectives

The chamber should be designed to create predictable airflow through the protected interior.

Primary objectives include:

- Deliver HEPA-filtered air uniformly into the chamber
- Establish airflow from the upper portion of the chamber toward lower exhaust locations
- Continuously purge particles generated or introduced inside the chamber
- Minimize turbulent recirculation
- Minimize stagnant or dead-air zones
- Prevent uncontrolled infiltration of surrounding room air when the doors are closed
- Maintain the intended cleanliness level around items placed on the internal rack
- Provide acceptable recovery after the access doors are opened and closed

The exhaust arrangement may use perforations, slots, grilles, or other controlled openings at the lower front, rear, and/or sides of the enclosure.

The final exhaust configuration should be based on airflow performance rather than packaging convenience alone.

## Filtration

The CEC will use HEPA filtration as the primary particulate-control method.

Engineering must define:

- HEPA filter efficiency
- Applicable filter standard
- Filter dimensions
- Filter orientation
- Filter face velocity
- Filter pressure drop
- Filter sealing method
- Filter replacement procedure
- Filter access location
- Filter service interval
- Filter integrity testing method
- Upstream pre-filtration requirements

The filter installation must prevent meaningful bypass around the HEPA filter.

## Blower System

The blower system must provide sufficient airflow to overcome pressure losses through:

- Intake openings
- Pre-filter, if used
- HEPA filter
- Internal ducting or plenum
- Supply-air distribution system
- Chamber
- Exhaust openings

The blower should be selected based on the complete system operating point rather than free-air CFM.

Engineering should evaluate:

- Required airflow volume
- Air velocity
- Static pressure
- Motor type
- Speed control
- Noise
- Vibration
- Heat generation
- Energy consumption
- Service life
- Clean-environment compatibility

Variable-speed control should be considered to allow airflow to be adjusted during development, balancing, validation, and potentially normal operation.

## Chamber Construction

### Exterior Enclosure

The preliminary enclosure construction is:

- 16-gauge cold-rolled steel sheet metal
- Formed and mechanically joined and/or welded construction
- White painted or powder-coated exterior finish

The final coating system must be compatible with the intended operating environment and routine cleaning procedures.

Engineering should minimize:

- Exposed seams
- Sharp edges
- Unnecessary horizontal ledges
- Dirt traps
- Difficult-to-clean joints
- Exposed unfinished carbon steel

### Interior Surfaces

The final interior material is to be determined.

Potential configurations include:

1. Painted or powder-coated CRS interior
2. Type 304 stainless steel interior panels with painted CRS exterior construction
3. All-Type 304 stainless steel premium construction

The interior should provide smooth, cleanable surfaces compatible with the intended cleanliness level.

A stainless steel interior should be strongly considered for models intended for higher-grade cleanroom applications.

---

## Internal Rack

The chamber will include an open wire rack or shelf for supporting components while allowing filtered air to move around the stored item.

The preliminary concept uses:

- Chrome-plated steel wire construction
- Removable rack
- Open-wire geometry
- Airflow through and around the rack

Engineering should evaluate Type 304 stainless steel wire construction as an alternative, particularly for higher-cleanliness or premium configurations.

The rack should be:

- Removable without specialized tools
- Easy to clean
- Resistant to corrosion
- Mechanically stable
- Designed to minimize airflow obstruction

Adjustable rack height should be considered.

---

## Access Doors

The CEC will include windowed doors providing access to the controlled chamber.

The preliminary design uses front-mounted doors with transparent viewing panels.

Door design objectives include:

- Clear visibility into the chamber
- Easy opening and closing
- Minimal obstruction of the work area
- Smooth, cleanable surfaces
- Reliable closed position
- Controlled gaps around the door perimeter
- Minimal particle generation from hinges and hardware
- Easy replacement of transparent panels if damaged

Potential transparent panel materials include:

- Tempered glass
- Laminated safety glass
- Polycarbonate
- Acrylic

The final material should be selected based on cleanliness, chemical resistance, scratch resistance, safety, weight, and cost.

Magnetic or mechanical catches may be used to retain the doors in the closed position.

---

## Door-Open Conditions and Recovery

Opening the chamber doors will disturb the controlled environment and allow surrounding room air to enter the chamber.

The product should therefore be designed around a defined recovery process.

A potential performance metric is:

**Recovery Time:** The elapsed time after the chamber doors are closed until the chamber returns to its specified cleanliness condition.

Recovery performance should be evaluated during prototype testing.

Factors affecting recovery include:

- Chamber volume
- Airflow rate
- Air changes
- Supply-air distribution
- Exhaust configuration
- Door size
- Door-open duration
- Surrounding room cleanliness
- Internal equipment or product geometry
- Particle generation inside the chamber

A validated recovery-time specification may become an important commercial performance characteristic of the CEC.

## Noise and Vibration

## ISO Classification and Validation

ISO cleanliness classification must be treated as a verified performance specification rather than an assumed characteristic of HEPA filtration.

If Eden intends to market a CEC as providing an ISO Class 5 environment, the production configuration must be tested using the applicable requirements of the ISO 14644 series and clearly defined operating conditions.

The validation plan should define conditions such as:

- Chamber empty or loaded
- Doors open or closed
- Blower operating state
- Fan-speed setting
- Measurement locations
- Particle sizes evaluated
- Surrounding room classification
- Test duration
- Recovery procedure
- Number of sampling locations
- Instrument requirements

Marketing materials must distinguish between:

- HEPA-filtered airflow
- Design target cleanliness
- Tested cleanliness
- Certified or validated cleanliness classification

No ISO classification should be stated as guaranteed until testing supports the claim.

## Development Priorities

The initial engineering effort should prioritize the following questions:

1. What internal chamber volume provides the best balance between usable space and airflow requirements?
2. What airflow pattern produces the most uniform protected zone?
3. Where should exhaust air leave the chamber?
4. What blower and HEPA filter combination provides the required operating point?
5. What chamber pressure relationship should be maintained relative to the surrounding room?
6. How quickly can the chamber recover after the doors are opened and closed?
7. Can the intended configuration reliably achieve the target ISO cleanliness classification?
8. How does a representative product load affect airflow and particle performance?
9. Which interior materials provide the required cleanability at an acceptable manufacturing cost?
10. What features are necessary for the first production model, and which should be reserved for future versions?
